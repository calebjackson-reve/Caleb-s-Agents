# The Seven Trust Principles (score any outbound 0 to 7)

**Source:** Nick Saraev, "COLD EMAIL COPYWRITING & OUTREACH Full Course 2026", chapters 2 to 3 (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uSTGNHGFOAo&t=236s)

## What Nick teaches

Outbound is one question: can you get a stranger with zero trust to say yes? Nick (behavioral neuroscience degree, about a decade of outbound, $15M+ in outbound sales) says none of this is magic. It is documented psychology, mostly organized by Robert Cialdini's book *Influence*. He lists seven principles, then uses them as a literal checklist to grade every email he roasts later in the course. An email gets a check or an X on each, so every message has a score out of 7.

1. **Give first.** Restaurant mints, Costco samples. Give something the reader values before you ask. In outbound this is an insight, a free asset, a fix. "I noticed XYZ is misconfigured on your landing page, I think you're losing $10 to 20K a month, let me show you how to fix it." You do not make the ask. The ask is inferred. Every message in the current year must give something.
2. **Micro commitments.** Never open with the big ask ("pay me $4,000"). Start tiny ("watch one minute of this video, if it's not useful never message me again"), then escalate: longer video, phone call, video call, close. The more times someone says yes before the ask, the more likely they say yes to the ask.
3. **Social proof.** Humans are herd animals. Show others taking the action. Three rules: use specific numbers ("$112,482 last week for an XYZ business"), name real people or companies where you can, and match the reference group (a result for a company that looks like theirs is worth far more than a result for a dog walker). Best modern outbound makes this a throwaway line.
4. **Authority.** Hyper relevant credentials or accomplishments, matched to the audience. "I'm a Google partner" landed with blue collar business owners where his neuroscience degree did not. Also signal confidence in the writing itself: no hedging ("I believe maybe I could help"), say "I can absolutely help, I just did this for X."
5. **Rapport.** Shared context, the more specific the better (same school, same city, both own Yorkshire terriers). Explicit rapport is saying it. Implicit rapport is mirroring: tone, message length, punctuation, in-group style (lowercase to SF VCs in 2022). Make them feel like you are sitting across from them at a bar.
6. **Scarcity.** Limit availability or set a real deadline ("proposal expires end of the week"). Make the constraint real and even self-deprecating: "I can only take on two of these this month because I'm juggling other client projects." Fake constraints trip people's BS detectors. Nick says this is the least used principle.
7. **Shared identity.** Common ground: same industry, same values, same struggle, in-group language. Highlight a shared hardship. This is the most important one in his view.

He is explicit that these overlap. You do not memorize them. You learn to recognize the patterns so you can produce them on demand.

## Step by step

1. Paste the draft. Identify sender, channel, and who the reader is.
2. Walk the seven principles in order and mark each as a check, a half, or an X with a one-line reason. Be strict: a "15-minute call" is not giving, it is taking. "We help companies" is not social proof. Calling yourself an AI is negative authority.
3. Total the score out of 7. Nick's own quick rewrites land around 5 to 6. Most inbound pitches he received scored 0 to 1.
4. For every X, write the single sentence that would flip it. Giving: what free asset or insight can be offered? Social proof: what specific number and matching reference client? Scarcity: what real capacity or date limit exists?
5. Rewrite using the ce-four-step-framework skill, then re-score. Stop when nothing scores an X and the message is still short enough to pass the "would a real person text this" test.

## Templates / prompts

```
SCORECARD for: <draft name>
1 Give first ........ [ ] what is the reader getting before any ask?
2 Micro commitment .. [ ] is the first ask tiny and the next step obvious?
3 Social proof ...... [ ] specific number + named or described client in THEIR reference group?
4 Authority ......... [ ] relevant credential or confident, unhedged claim?
5 Rapport ........... [ ] shared context + mirrored tone and length?
6 Scarcity .......... [ ] real time or capacity limit?
7 Shared identity ... [ ] in-group language, same industry or struggle?
Score: _/7
Fixes: one sentence per X
```

Real estate scoring example (seller outreach):
```
"Hi, I'm a Realtor with Keller Williams. I'd love to help you sell your home. Call me anytime."
Give 0, Micro 0, Proof 0, Authority 0.5, Rapport 0, Scarcity 0, Identity 0 = 0.5/7

"Hey Sarah, saw the Tudor on Royal St finally got a new roof, looks sharp. I sold the Pearson place two doors down in 11 days last spring for $14K over list. Not pitching you on listing, but I pulled a quick 1-page number on what yours would bring today. Want me to drop it in your mailbox Thursday? If so just reply 'yes'."
Give 1, Micro 1, Proof 1, Authority 1, Rapport 1, Scarcity 0.5, Identity 0.5 = 6/7
```

## Pitfalls Nick calls out

- Giving nothing and calling it giving. A call, a link, or a survey takes time, it does not give.
- Vague social proof ("I've helped a lot of people") versus a specific number for a lookalike client.
- Credentials the audience does not care about. Match authority to the person in front of you.
- Fake urgency. Readers have good spam detectors.
- Trying to score 7/7 on every message. Nick usually skips or goes light on scarcity and says that is fine.

## How Caleb should use this

- Run the scorecard on every expired listing letter, FSBO text, absentee owner mailer, and investor email before it goes out. Sending anything is "needs Caleb's approval before running."
- Build a "proof bank" for AIRE: specific numbers (days on market, over-list percentages, number of St. Francisville and Baton Rouge closings, named neighborhoods) so social proof is always concrete and matched to the recipient's area and price band.
- Rapport for Caleb is easy: local landmarks, parish names, LSU, the Audubon Pilgrimage, hurricane season. Use real local context, not generic flattery.
- Real scarcity Caleb actually has: a limited number of listing appointments per week, an open house date, a buyer already looking in that subdivision.
- Give first ideas that are lawful for a licensee: a neighborhood sales snapshot, a one-page "what your home would bring" range, a repair-before-listing checklist.

## Verification

- Every draft has a written score and a reason per principle.
- No principle is marked as a check without a quoted phrase from the draft that earns it.
- The rewrite scores at least 4/7 and is no longer than the original unless the original had no offer at all.
