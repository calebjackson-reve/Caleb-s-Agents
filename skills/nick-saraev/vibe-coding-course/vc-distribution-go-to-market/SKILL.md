# Distribution is the moat: outbound, inbound, affiliate

**Source:** Nick Saraev, "VIBE CODING FULL COURSE: Gemini 3.1 + Antigravity (6 Hrs)", chapter 47 (https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=gcuR_-rzlDw&t=21240s)

## What Nick teaches

### Software is no longer the moat
Thirty or forty years ago software was hard to make, so a better operating system or Photoshop was a durable edge. Now anyone with a few ideas, agency and $15 to $20 can build anything, and there will soon be "100 million times as many software engineers in the form of AI agents." Netflix survives not because its code is special but because it owns **distribution**: customers, vendors, relationships, staff, culture. "The only thing that makes your software different from somebody else's software is the customers." Play the distribution playbook, not the software playbook.

### The 60-second clock
If your business's life is a 60-second sweep, spend **3 or 4 seconds building** and the rest marketing. The old build-in-public rule was 50/50. "That no longer applies. You need to spend 19 to 20 times as much time marketing as building." Building is the ticket to the ride, marketing is the ride. "Without marketing and sales you don't have a product, you have a hobby."

### Launch today, learn from reality
Builders are "giant nerds" whose opinions overlap maybe 50% with what users want. Only customers give ground truth. "Launch your vibe-coded app today, contact 20 people, have all of them use it for free, and have them tell you what they liked, didn't like, wished it did." It is the 20th version that makes money, not the first. Maximize customer experience over polish, get as much distribution as fast as possible, then iterate on the product.

### Three ways to market
1. **Outbound** (the sniper rifle): go directly to people who need it. Cold email (templated but reads one-to-one: "Hey Pete, love what you're doing at XYZ. You're spending $ on [software], I can save that with [product], happy to give you a major discount as one of my first five signups, here's a short video") and cold DMs (LinkedIn for B2B, Instagram). Works best B2B because the lead sources (LinkedIn) are commercial by nature. It is the fastest and the hardest, "you put your ego on the line." Nick's first business was literally knocking on doors and getting chased out of plazas, and it made his first $150k to $200k.
2. **Inbound** (the megaphone): create valuable content, build an audience, send a slice to paid products. Slower. Everyone else has a megaphone too and the big accounts have bigger ones, so beginners "screech into the void" and quit before momentum. Pays off later.
3. **Affiliate** (borrowed distribution): pay someone with an audience (he uses himself as the example) 30% to market it for you. Faster and more predictable than building your own, not necessarily cheaper. Inbound affiliates scale best. Available at any stage.

### The curves
Outbound: money early, then plateau as each send costs your time. Inbound: nothing for a long time, then a steep climb once the brand catches. Blend: mostly outbound at the start, shift toward inbound over time, so you get revenue now without the ceiling later. His threshold: once you have "maybe $10, $20, $30k a month in recurring revenue" from outbound, start building the brand.

### High vs low ticket
High-ticket SaaS means demos and sales calls. Low-ticket means self-serve onboarding and more marketing. Pick on purpose.

## Step by step

1. Write the one-sentence promise and the ROI sentence (from `vc-value-based-pricing`).
2. Ship the current version. Do not polish first.
3. List 20 people who have the problem. Offer free use in exchange for feedback. Collect what they liked, did not like, wished for. Build v2 from that, not from your assumptions.
4. Outbound engine: lead list (scrape or hand-build), a personalized template with a discount for early users and a short demo video, send daily. Track replies and demos.
5. Once revenue is steady, start one inbound channel with consistent posting, expecting a long ramp.
6. In parallel, approach 2 or 3 people with an audience of your buyers and offer a revenue share.
7. Review monthly: time split (aim 5% build / 95% market), replies, signups, churn, feedback themes.

## Templates / prompts

Cold email (first-five discount):
```
Subject: quick one, {{first_name}}

Hey {{first_name}}, love what you're doing at {{company}}. Noticed you're
likely spending {{current_tool_or_hours}} on {{job}}. I built {{product}}
to handle that in {{time}}, and since you'd be one of my first five
signups I'd give it to you for {{discount}}. Short video of how it works:
{{link}}. Happy to help even if it's not a fit.
Caleb
```

Beta feedback ask (20 free users):
```
I'm giving {{product}} to 20 people free for 30 days. All I ask is one
10-minute call or a few lines on: what you liked, what you didn't, what
you wish it did, and whether it solved {{problem}}. Interested?
```

Affiliate pitch:
```
Your audience is exactly who {{product}} is for. Rather than you building
something, use mine, and I'll pay you {{30}}% of every subscription you
send, tracked with your own link. Want a login to try it first?
```

Monthly go-to-market review:
```
Build hours: ___  Marketing hours: ___  (target 1:19)
Outbound sent: ___  replies: ___  demos: ___  closed: ___
Inbound posts: ___  followers delta: ___  signups from content: ___
Affiliate partners: ___  referred signups: ___
Top 3 feedback themes -> v{{n+1}} changes:
```

## Pitfalls Nick calls out

- Building the "incredible solution nobody else can do" is a low-probability bet now.
- Designing from your own opinions instead of customer feedback.
- Starting with inbound and quitting before momentum.
- Treating marketing as something to do after the product is "ready."
- Over-polishing the app when the product is only 5% of the pie.
